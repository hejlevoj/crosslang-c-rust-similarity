\begin{abstract}
C-to-Rust migration is an active agenda, but no dataset pairs the two
languages at the level of whole solutions to a shared problem. We present
\dsname, 1857 C/Rust implementation pairs drawn from competitive programming
archives, normalised, deduplicated, and stratified into difficulty tiers by
zero-shot cross-language embedding similarity, with per-record provenance and
licensing. Cross-language retrieval is hard on this data, and harder than a
single baseline would suggest: TF-IDF over shared identifiers reaches
MRR $0.21$ and so beats a pretrained code model at $0.19$, which on the
hardest quarter of the data scores $1.7\times$ chance. Finetuning reaches
$0.79$, and lifts that hardest quarter $10\times$. Renaming every user
identifier settles what is being learned: the finetuned models keep 97\% of
their accuracy without them, while the pretrained model keeps 66\% --- less
than the lexical baseline does --- and on structurally divergent pairs the
finetuned models are \emph{better} without identifiers than with them.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

Memory-safety defects remain the dominant vulnerability class in C. Microsoft
has for two decades attributed roughly $70\%$ of its CVEs to that
class~\cite{russinovich2025rustconf}, and Android shows the substitution
works: its annual memory-safety vulnerabilities fell from 223 in 2019 to under
50 in 2024 as Rust adoption grew, with roughly $1000\times$ lower defect
density in Rust than in C and C++~\cite{google2025android}. Policy has
followed, through CISA's memory-safe roadmaps~\cite{cisa2023roadmaps} and the
US cyber director's call to adopt memory-safe
languages~\cite{oncd2024memory}; DARPA's TRACTOR
programme~\cite{darpa2024tractor} funds the automated translation of legacy C
to Rust outright, on the premise that the codebases that matter are too large
to port by hand.

Translation tools are consequently proliferating, and they are evaluated almost
entirely on whether their output compiles and passes the original test
suite~\cite{nitin2025c2saferrust,shetty2025syzygy}. That is the right primary
criterion. It is also incomplete --- a translation can pass every test and
still be an unfaithful rendering of its source --- and it is unavailable
precisely where it is most needed, since legacy C is what most often lacks
tests. The complementary question, \emph{are these two programs, in two
languages, the same program?}, is a similarity judgement rather than an
execution one, and answering it with a learned model requires paired
supervision.

No such corpus exists for this language pair. The parallel code datasets either
span many languages at snippet
granularity~\cite{zhu2022xlcost,zhu2022cost} or go deep on a single pair that
is not this one~\cite{ahmad2023avatar}, and the largest collections are
grouped by problem rather than aligned across
languages~\cite{puri2021codenet,lu2021codexglue}. None of them includes Rust.

We present \dsname, 1857 pairs of C and Rust implementations of the same
competitive programming problem, each with its statement, normalised entry
points, per-record provenance and licence, a difficulty tier derived from
zero-shot cross-language embedding similarity, and a frozen stratified split.

Measuring on it reframes what the task requires. A lexical baseline---TF-IDF
over shared identifiers, no model---beats a pretrained code model zero-shot,
because competitive programmers reuse variable names across languages. Renaming
every user identifier separates the regimes: the pretrained model loses a third
of its accuracy, more than the lexical baseline does, while finetuned models
lose almost nothing. Cross-language matching here is learnable, but not from
identifiers.

\noindent\textbf{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item \dsname, 1857 C/Rust solution pairs with problem statements,
        normalised entry points, per-record provenance and licensing.
  \item A difficulty stratification derived from zero-shot cross-language
        embedding similarity, independent of the model family used in
        evaluation, which we show transfers to a different family.
  \item Retrieval baselines per difficulty tier over a frozen stratified
        split, including a lexical baseline and an identifier ablation that
        together establish what the task does and does not reduce to.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Datasets}
\label{sec:related}

Parallel code corpora divide by what they align and how many languages they
span, and Table~\ref{tab:related} places \dsname{} among them.

The largest are not parallel at all. CodeNet~\cite{puri2021codenet} collects
roughly 14 million submissions across 55 languages from AtCoder and AIZU, and
POJ-104, distributed as part of CodeXGLUE~\cite{lu2021codexglue}, holds student
C++ submissions to 104 problems. Both group solutions by problem, so pairs can
be derived, but neither ships an aligned bilingual corpus and both are
dominated by C, C++, Java and Python.

Among the explicitly parallel corpora, xCodeEval~\cite{khan2023xcodeeval} is
the broadest, with 25 million Codeforces examples over 17 languages and
execution-based evaluation through unit tests.
XLCoST~\cite{zhu2022xlcost} and CoST~\cite{zhu2022cost} align at the finer
granularity of code snippets rather than whole programs, across 7 languages
drawn from GeeksForGeeks; the alignment is what makes them valuable and also
what restricts them to the languages that site covers.
AVATAR~\cite{ahmad2023avatar} takes the opposite tack and goes deep on one
pair, 9{,}515 Java/Python problems with unit tests for a 250-example subset.

None of them includes Rust. That is the gap \dsname{} fills, and the reason it
is a single language pair rather than a broad matrix: the pair that matters for
the migration pressure described in Section~\ref{sec:usecases} is C to Rust
specifically, and no existing corpus aligns those two at the level of whole
solutions to a shared problem.

The modelling literature this dataset is meant to serve is correspondingly
single-pair-poor. Unsupervised program translation was established on
C++/Java/Python by TransCoder~\cite{roziere2020transcoder}, and the pretrained
code encoders that cross-language retrieval is built
on---CodeBERT~\cite{feng2020codebert} and
UniXcoder~\cite{guo2022unixcoder}---were pretrained on corpora in which Rust is
marginal or absent. Cross-language clone detection has the same shape:
CLCDSA~\cite{nafi2019clcdsa} detects type-4 clones across Java, C\# and Python
using syntactic features and API-call similarity, and its evaluation, like the
corpora above, stops short of Rust.

On the C-to-Rust side specifically, the recent work is on \emph{producing}
Rust rather than on judging whether the result corresponds to its source.
C2SaferRust~\cite{nitin2025c2saferrust} decomposes C2Rust's unsafe output into
slices an LLM rewrites more safely, and Syzygy~\cite{shetty2025syzygy} pairs
LLM translation with dynamic analysis to keep code and tests consistent. Both
are evaluated by compilation and test-passing, which is the criterion
Section~\ref{sec:usecases} argues is necessary and not sufficient.

\section{Construction}
\label{sec:construction}

% ~0.75 page. Figure 1 is the pipeline with counts at each stage.

\subsection{Sources and pairing}

\dsname{} draws on two competitive programming archives: xCodeEval
\cite{khan2023xcodeeval}, itself derived from Codeforces, and IBM Project
CodeNet \cite{puri2021codenet}, derived from AtCoder. Both distribute accepted
submissions, so the two implementations of a pair are known to have passed the
same official tests. A pair is one problem, one accepted C submission, and one
accepted Rust submission.

\subsection{Normalisation and cleaning}

Both sides pass through a static analysis pipeline that strips comments,
removes unused functions and macros, normalises encoding, and applies the
language's standard formatter. Entry points are renamed to \texttt{solution} in
both languages so the function name carries no signal---which also means the
distributed code no longer compiles standalone
(Section~\ref{sec:limitations}).


From an initial 2013 pairs, 127 were removed: 2 confirmed mis-pairs, 69 AtCoder
easy/hard variants with near-identical statements (description embedding
similarity $>0.90$), 54 further suspicious pairs ($0.80$--$0.90$) found the same
way with OpenAI \texttt{text-embedding-3-large}, 1 length-ratio outlier and 1
case of genuine implementation divergence. A further 29 textbook reference
implementations were dropped: they carried the only copyleft content and the
only records whose halves were under different licences
(Section~\ref{sec:availability}), and had no problem statement. This leaves
1857 pairs.

\subsection{Difficulty stratification}

Each pair is scored by the cosine similarity between zero-shot
\texttt{SFR-Embedding-Code-400M\_R} embeddings of its C and Rust sides, and
binned at the 25th and 75th percentiles into easy, medium and hard
(thresholds $0.7856$ and $0.8687$; similarity mean $0.826$, standard deviation
$0.058$). The raw score is distributed alongside the tier, so the cuts can be
redrawn without re-running the model. Pairs a
generic code model already embeds close together are near-literal translations;
pairs it separates diverge structurally and demand a real cross-language
remapping.

The choice of SFR rather than UniXcoder~\cite{guo2022unixcoder} is
deliberate. Defining the tiers with
the same model family that is later finetuned would make hard pairs hard by
construction, and any reported per-tier improvement would be circular. SFR is
trained on a different corpus, so the tiers remain an independent axis. The
stratification is also origin-agnostic: AtCoder and Codeforces publish
difficulty ratings on incompatible scales, so upstream ratings are not used.

tier, so the quantile cuts can be redrawn without re-running the model.

\subsection{Statistics}

Solutions are short: a median of 516 characters of C and 749 of Rust, with
problem statements at a median of 1231. The Rust side runs about $1.5\times$
longer in characters (median ratio $1.50$) while being \emph{shorter} in lines
---31.4 against 33.6 on average---so Rust solutions pack more into each line.
A length-based heuristic therefore does not separate the two languages the way
the character counts alone would suggest.


Source and difficulty are not independent. CodeNet contributes 241 easy, 408
medium and 190 hard pairs; xCodeEval 224, 520 and 274. The CodeNet portion
skews easy while
the xCodeEval portion skews hard. Reporting a per-tier result therefore
requires a split that controls for both.

\subsection{Splits}

The train/validation/test assignment is frozen in the data rather than
regenerated from a seed, and is stratified by source $\times$ difficulty at
80/10/10: 1485, 185 and 187 pairs. The test set mirrors the full dataset to
within 0.3 percentage points by source and 0.6 by tier.

\section{Baselines}
\label{sec:baselines}

Retrieval is C-to-Rust unless stated otherwise: each C implementation queries
the pool of all 187 Rust implementations in the test split, and the target is
the one solving the same problem.

We report mean reciprocal rank, introduced for the TREC-8 question answering
track~\cite{voorhees1999trec8} and the standard measure for code search since
CodeSearchNet~\cite{husain2019codesearchnet} and
CodeXGLUE~\cite{lu2021codexglue}: each query contributes the reciprocal of the
rank at which its correct counterpart appears, so the score is dominated by
whether the answer lands at the top rather than merely somewhere in the list.
MRR@10 is the same quantity truncated, contributing zero when the rank exceeds
ten. Both are given because they diverge exactly where many positives fall
outside the top ten, which is the zero-shot regime and therefore the
denominator of every improvement ratio below. Chance at this pool size is
$\mathrm{MRR}=0.031$. LoRA figures are mean $\pm$ standard deviation over four
seeds; the other rows are single runs.

The lexical row is the one that fixes the frame. TF-IDF over shared
identifiers, with no model at all, reaches $0.206$ and so \emph{beats}
zero-shot UniXcoder~\cite{guo2022unixcoder} at $0.189$. Competitive programmers reuse variable names
across languages: \texttt{n} is shared by 85 of the 187 test pairs,
\texttt{i} by 84, then \texttt{a}, \texttt{t}, \texttt{s}, \texttt{j}. The
claim this dataset supports is therefore not that a generic code model finds
the task hard, but the stronger one that \emph{neither} generic code
embeddings \emph{nor} lexical matching solve it, and finetuning does ---
$3.8\times$ over zero-shot for LoRA~\cite{hu2022lora} and $4.2\times$ for
full finetuning
($4.2\times$ and $4.7\times$ on MRR@10, which diverges from MRR because many
zero-shot positives fall outside the top ten).

Three things follow, and together they are the strongest evidence that this
dataset measures what it is meant to.

\textbf{Retrieval is monotone in tier for every configuration.} The tiers were
cut with SFR while the models here are UniXcoder-based, so the ordering is a
property of the pairs rather than an artefact of the encoder that defined it.
Zero-shot UniXcoder reaches $0.053$ on the hard tier --- $1.7\times$ chance,
with exactly one of the 47 hard pairs at rank 1. A generic code model
effectively cannot solve a quarter of this dataset.

\textbf{Finetuning learns structure, not names.} The finetuned models keep
96--97\% of their MRR when identifiers are destroyed; zero-shot UniXcoder
keeps 66\%, which is \emph{less} than the 76\% the lexical baseline keeps. A
pretrained code model is more dependent on variable names than TF-IDF is.
That inverts the natural reading of Table~\ref{tab:baselines}: zero-shot
UniXcoder does not merely fail to beat lexical matching, it is doing a worse
version of the same thing.

\textbf{On hard pairs, identifiers are a liability.} Both finetuned models do
\emph{better} without them --- LoRA from $0.388$ to $0.429$, full finetuning
from $0.550$ to $0.611$, both about $+11\%$ --- while the lexical baseline and
the zero-shot model get worse. Where implementations diverge structurally, a
shared name is more often a coincidence than a signal, and a model trained
without that cue learns the remapping instead of leaning on it.

\subsection{Direction and parameter efficiency}

Querying with Rust beats querying with C (MRR $0.189$ to $0.233$ zero-shot,
$0.792$ to $0.836$ full), and the asymmetry nearly vanishes under the ablation
($0.768$ against $0.773$), locating it in identifiers rather than structure.
LoRA reaches $91\%$ of full finetuning overall with $0.23\%$ of the parameters,
but $98\%$ on easy pairs against $71\%$ on hard: the budget binds where the
remapping is non-trivial, and the aggregate ratio conceals it.

\section{Use Cases}
\label{sec:usecases}

Section~\ref{sec:intro} set out why C-to-Rust translation is being
funded rather than merely discussed. What follows is the gap between that
effort and what it currently measures.

\subsection{What a paired corpus contributes}

Translation tools are evaluated almost entirely on whether their output
compiles and passes the original tests. TRACTOR's own benchmark is a battery of
150 C programs scored for correctness and safety~\cite{llnl2024tractorbench}.
That is the right primary criterion and an incomplete one: a translation can
pass every test and still be an unfaithful or unidiomatic rendering of the
source, and a test suite is exactly what legacy C most often lacks. The
complementary question --- \emph{are these two programs, in two languages, the
same program?} --- is a similarity judgement, and it is the one \dsname{}
supplies supervision for.

Four concrete uses follow.

\textbf{Scoring translator output without tests.} A model finetuned on
\dsname{} assigns a cross-language similarity to a (C, Rust) pair. Applied to a
translator's input and output it gives a test-free signal that flags
translations drifting from their source --- usable as a regression check across
tool versions, or to rank candidates when a tool emits several. The per-tier
results in Section~\ref{sec:baselines} matter here: the usable range of such a
score is set by how well a model does on \emph{structurally divergent} pairs,
which is where the hard tier shows the current ceiling is $0.55$ MRR, not the
$0.98$ the easy tier would suggest.

\textbf{Finding an existing port.} Migrating a C codebase rarely starts from
nothing: equivalent functionality often already exists in Rust, in a crate or
in a sibling project. Locating it is cross-language retrieval over whole
implementations, which is the task this dataset measures directly.

\textbf{Cross-language clone and vulnerability tracking.} When a CVE is filed
against a C implementation, the question of whether a Rust port inherits the
defect --- or whether a Rust crate is a translation of vulnerable C --- is a
type-4 clone problem across languages. Type-4 clone corpora are overwhelmingly
single-language; a paired C/Rust corpus gives the cross-language case
supervision it has lacked.

\subsection{What it does not support}

The pairs are competitive programming solutions: short, single-file,
I/O-driven, and free of the allocation patterns, pointer aliasing and
concurrency that make real C hard to port. Results here should be read as a
measurement on a clean proxy for the cross-language matching problem, not as
evidence about systems code, which is where the migration pressure described
above actually sits. Section~\ref{sec:limitations} returns to this.

\section{Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

\textbf{Equivalence is inherited, not verified.} No tests or expected I/O
are distributed. That both submissions were accepted upstream is evidence of
functional equivalence, not a check of it, and the entry-point renaming means
the code does not compile standalone. Execution-based verification is the
clearest line of future work.

\textbf{Competitive programming is not production code.} Solutions are short,
single-file and I/O-driven; nothing here should be assumed to transfer to
systems code, which is where the migration pressure actually sits.

\textbf{Upstream identifiers are absent}, so pairs cannot be traced to the
originating submission, and \textbf{difficulty is a proxy} for embedding
similarity rather than algorithmic difficulty or translation effort.

\textbf{Variance is measured for one configuration.} The LoRA rows are means
over four seeds ($\sigma = 0.011$ on MRR); the others are single runs, so that
spread is the only direct evidence of how much of any gap is seed noise.

\textbf{An earlier release's tier labels were not exactly reproducible.}
Regenerating them matches the aggregate distribution (mean $0.826$ against a
reported $0.823$) but only 89\% of individual labels, with disagreement spread
symmetrically across adjacent tiers---a pattern threshold movement alone cannot
produce. The labels released here are the regenerated ones, and the raw scores
now ship alongside them. \textbf{Pre-training contamination is also possible},
both archives being public.
